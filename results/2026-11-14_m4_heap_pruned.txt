## verdicts under the pre-registered rules

H56, supported, all four parts.
  (a) X(heap_indexed, depth) / X(array, depth) still at least 1.5: BX 2.299
      [2.271, 2.318], synthetic 2.690 [2.669, 2.717]. Supported.
  (b) On the synthetic feed that ratio at most half of the fifteenth run's
      11.563, so at most 5.78: 2.690, which is 23 percent of it. Supported.
  (c) On BX within a quarter of the fifteenth run's 2.253, so between 1.69
      and 2.82: 2.299, a change of 2.0 percent. Supported.
  (d) At least half of the orders arriving at the best price on the real
      feed gone within one second: 0.5388 of the 3,424,615 that ended inside
      the window. Supported, and robust to the obvious objection: 42,994
      orders were still resting when the window ended and are excluded, and
      even if every one of them would have outlived a second the share is
      0.5321, still above a half.

So the explanation written into the pre-registration was right, and that is
what (b) and (c) were for. BX held about five live orders an instrument, so
pruning found almost nothing to skip and its ratio moved 2 percent; the
synthetic feed held about two thousand an instrument, so its ratio fell to
less than a quarter of what the unpruned scan had made it look. The
fifteenth run's depth figures were an artifact of our implementation, and
these are the ones to quote.

Per message above decoding alone, on the real BX feed, with every structure
at its best:

  array, best price only                 94.87 cycles   187 instructions
  array, best price and depth            94.92          187
  heap_indexed, best price only         161.13          344
  heap_indexed, best price and depth    218.25          511
  heap_lazy, best price only            195.47          387
  heap_lazy, best price and depth       334.33          677

The headline stands and is now honest on both sides: a sorted array of
aggregated price levels answers the best price 1.70x cheaper than a binary
heap of orders, answers depth for nothing where the heap pays 2.30x, and the
two array stages differ by 0.000 of the total because they are the same
operation.

## which deletion policy wins depends on the feed, and reverses

Under the unpruned scan the lazy heap looked worse than the indexed one for
depth on both feeds. With the query at its best the ordering reverses on one
of them:

                          BX (real)        synthetic
  lazy depth / indexed      1.532            0.792
  lazy top / indexed top    1.213            0.573

The mechanism is in the counters rather than in the timings. The indexed
policy writes a position back into the order table at every step of every
sift, so it pays in proportion to how deep the heaps are; the lazy policy
pays only when a dead entry reaches the top, so it pays in proportion to
churn. The synthetic feed ended 1,766,807 orders over 5,000,000 messages and
popped 50,547 stale entries; BX ended 4,386,147 over 10,000,000 and popped
4,200,776, eighty times as many. Deep books with little churn favour the
lazy policy; shallow books with heavy churn favour the indexed one. Neither
choice is right in general, which is worth more than either number.

## fleeting quotes, measured

The third source brief recommends posting quotes "for extremely short
duration (milliseconds)". On the real feed, of the orders that arrived at
the best price on their side and ended inside the window:

  within 100 us   2.71%      within 100 ms   32.09%
  within 1 ms     9.94%      within 1 s      53.88%
  within 10 ms   20.66%      mean            21.6 s

So the advice describes a minority of real orders rather than the norm: one
in ten lasts less than a millisecond, and the mean is twenty seconds because
the tail is long. 78.08 percent of the orders that ended had arrived at the
best price, which on a venue with a thin book counts an order that improves
the quote as well as one that joins it. Lifetimes are from each message's
own timestamp; orders still resting at the end of the window are excluded,
and that exclusion is 0.97 percent of those tracked.

## a measurement note worth carrying into the paper

The array book's code did not change between the fifteenth and sixteenth
runs, and its cost moved: 98.59 cycles per message above decoding in the
first, 94.87 in the second, a drop of 3.8 percent, while within each run the
bootstrap interval is about half a percent wide. The binary changed around
it, so this is the code-layout sensitivity that H48's alignment build
probed, showing up between two runs of the same experiment. It is why the
quantity to quote is a ratio measured inside one run, not an absolute cycle
count carried between them, and every ratio above is from a single run.
