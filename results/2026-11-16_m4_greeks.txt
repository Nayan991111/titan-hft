What a pricing kernel costs (H65)
Apple M4 MacBook Air (Mac16,13), macOS 26B123, rustc 1.92.0, commit a08c9e2,
2026-11-16 10:09 IST. AC power confirmed by pmset, Low Power Mode 0, Docker not running,
no profiler attached. 30 independent launches of `titan-greeks 3 ui pmu` and 30 of the same
with `bg`, 3 epochs per launch, 7 arms, 2 strike ladders, 4096 contracts per arm per epoch,
64 strikes per chain call. 2520 measured lines. The launch is the unit of observation and
every pair is formed inside one launch.

The kernel was validated before it was measured, by eight tests whose oracles share none of
its machinery: the cumulative normal against compensated Simpson integration of the density
with the tail integrated outward (worst absolute error 7.448e-8 against the 7.5e-8 the
approximation claims), every sensitivity against a central difference of a price built on
that integrated normal (worst deviation 0.352 of a tolerance whose absolute part is the
difference's own cancellation floor), put-call parity to 4.441e-16, the fused kernel against
six separate functions to 2.165e-16, and the strike ladder against the scalar kernel to
5.684e-16.

H65 (e) REFUTED, and it refutes the premise this run was built on. The registered claim was
    that the fused kernel exceeds 135 cycles per contract, the cycles per message of the
    whole single-core trading path, in at least 21 of 30 launches. It exceeds it in 0 of 30.
    The median is 58.733 cycles, bootstrap interval [58.668, 58.800].

    So a Black-Scholes price with all five first-order sensitivities costs 58.7 cycles and
    13.9 ns on a performance core, which is 43 percent of the 135 cycles the entire
    feed-to-decision path takes on real BX data. The reasoning that motivated this run, that
    a real options decision would blow the per-message budget and so the measured path is
    unrepresentative, is wrong by a factor of 2.3 in the opposite direction. On this machine
    an options market maker can afford to price on every message. A full 64-strike ladder
    reprices in 58.04 x 64 = 3715 cycles, about 832 ns at the 4.47 GHz this arm ran at.

    The 135 figure is flagged PRELIMINARY PROTOCOL in the experiments table, so this is a
    comparison against a fixed number from an earlier run rather than a paired measurement.
    That weakens the comparison but not the conclusion: the gap is 2.3x, far outside the
    1.364x noise floor measured in H62, and the kernel's absolute cycles are recorded here so
    the comparison can be redone when the path is re-measured under the launch protocol.

(a) SUPPORTED. The six separate functions cost 2.284x the fused kernel in cycles per
    contract, straddling ladder, performance cores, 30 of 30 launches, sign p < 0.0001,
    median difference +75.343 cycles [+75.275, +75.445]. On efficiency cores the ratio rises
    to 2.426. The instruction counts say why: 748.19 against 298.78, a factor of 2.50, since
    each separate function recomputes d1, d2, both discount exponentials and up to two
    cumulative normals. Reuse of the shared terms is worth 75 cycles per contract, and it
    costs nothing in accuracy: the two agree to 2.165e-16.

(b) SUPPORTED. The cumulative normal alone is 0.270 of the fused kernel in cycles,
    straddling ladder, performance cores, 0 of 30 launches above the fused cost, sign
    p < 0.0001. At 15.87 cycles for one evaluation and two evaluations plus one density per
    fused call, the transcendental is the dominant term, as registered.

(c) REFUTED, and the mechanism I registered was wrong. The claim was that scalar single
    precision does not cut cycles per contract by more than 10 percent, so the ratio would
    stand above 0.90. It is 0.750 on performance cores, 0 of 30 launches above, sign
    p < 0.0001: 44.06 cycles against 58.73. The registration disclosed that an x86 smoke test
    had already measured 0.867, on the refuting side, and the M4 is further from the
    threshold still.

    My reasoning was that the code is scalar, so no vector width is gained, and the operation
    count is identical on a narrower type. The instruction counts show where that reasoning
    failed: 254.17 against 298.78, a reduction of 15 percent in instructions, so the saving
    is not in the arithmetic width at all but in shorter library routines. The single-precision
    exponential and logarithm evaluate shorter polynomials than their double counterparts.
    The remaining saving beyond instruction count comes from a higher IPC, 5.77 against 5.09.
    On efficiency cores the ratio is 0.826, so the benefit is smaller there.

    Paired with the accuracy measured in the validation tests, worst relative departure from
    double of 3.3e-7 on price and 2.5e-5 on gamma, this is a quotable tradeoff rather than a
    recommendation: 25 percent fewer cycles for five decimal digits on the second derivative.

(d) REFUTED on performance cores and reversed on efficiency cores, exactly as the
    registration said it might be. The claim was that hoisting the strike-independent terms
    by hand cuts cycles per contract below 0.90 of the per-contract loop. The measured ratio
    is 0.976 on performance cores, with the hand-hoisted form cheaper in 27 of 30 launches,
    sign p < 0.0001, a saving of 1.427 cycles out of 59.5. Statistically certain and
    practically nothing.

    The registration predicted this and named the reason: LLVM already hoists those terms out
    of the per-contract loop at opt-level 3 with fat LTO, because the base contract is
    loop-invariant in every field except the strike. The instruction counts confirm it from
    the other side: the hand-hoisted form issues 303.59 instructions against 296.78, slightly
    more, because it carries the chunk bookkeeping and a per-strike validity test the scalar
    loop does not need. The finding is about the compiler, not the kernel.

    On efficiency cores it reverses: the ratio is 1.021 with the hand-hoisted form more
    expensive in 28 of 30 launches, sign p < 0.0001. So hand-hoisting is worth 2.4 percent on
    one cluster and costs 2.1 percent on the other.

(f) SUPPORTED by a factor of 32 over its threshold, and the magnitude is the point rather
    than the ratio. The claim was that the straddling ladder costs at least 2.0x the
    out-of-the-money ladder in branch mispredicts per contract in the fused arm. The measured
    ratio is 64.4 on performance cores, 30 of 30 launches, sign p < 0.0001.

    The absolute numbers: 0.0322 mispredicts per contract against 0.0005. The cumulative
    normal branches on the sign of its argument; the straddling ladder crosses the spot so d1
    changes sign along it, the out-of-the-money ladder does not. A 64x ratio sounds decisive
    and the cost is 0.31 cycles out of 58.73, half of one percent, which is what the arms
    actually differ by (58.73 straddling against 58.42 out of the money). This is worth
    stating precisely because a ratio reported without its magnitude would read as an
    important effect, and it is not one.

    One observation here is unexplained and is recorded as open. The `price` arm mispredicts
    0.0635 per contract, twice the fused arm's 0.0322, although both evaluate the same two
    cumulative normals. Its straddling-to-out-of-the-money cycle difference is also larger,
    3.12 cycles against 0.31. Why the larger fused body predicts better is not established by
    this run; if-conversion to conditional selects in one and not the other would explain it
    but was not measured.

THE CORE CLOCK IS NOT A CONSTANT OF THE MACHINE, and this run settles it with a wider spread
than H64 showed. The performance cores ran this compute-bound kernel at 4.05 to 4.61 GHz,
against 3.506 to 3.628 GHz for the cross-core round trip in H64 and the 3.3 to 4.4 GHz
recorded earlier. So the same core is 27 percent faster in clock on a compute kernel than on
a latency-bound handoff. Every cycles-to-nanoseconds conversion in the paper must use the
measured clock of its own arm, which every table here reports per case.

The efficiency cores held 1.088 GHz across every arm, matching H64's 1.080, with one
exception that is recorded as a caveat rather than a result: the `separate` arm on
efficiency cores reported 1.542 GHz on the straddling ladder and 1.083 on the
out-of-the-money one, and its wall-time interval is correspondingly wide, +102.808 ns
[+67.605, +168.150]. That arm is the heaviest and runs longest, so the efficiency cluster
appears to boost under it, unevenly. No wall-time figure for `separate` on efficiency cores
should be quoted as a point estimate from this run.

CYCLES AND NANOSECONDS AGREE HERE, unlike in H64. Every pairing measured in both units gives
the same ratio to within a few percent: separate against fused is 2.284 in cycles and 2.300
in nanoseconds, single against double precision 0.750 and 0.747, hand-hoisted against the
loop 0.976 and 0.976. H64's divergence between the two units was specific to a latency-bound
cross-core handoff where the clock differed between arms; in a compute-bound kernel on one
core the two units carry the same information.

IPC runs from 4.93 to 5.78 across every arm on performance cores, so this kernel issues close
to five instructions per cycle sustained, with L1D misses below 0.017 per contract everywhere
and no arm above 0.12 even on efficiency cores. The kernel is arithmetic-bound, not
memory-bound, which is the regime a closed-form pricer should be in and is worth stating
because it means the result above is a statement about the core's arithmetic throughput
rather than about this machine's memory system.
