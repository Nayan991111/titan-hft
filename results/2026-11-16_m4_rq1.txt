H68 (d) FAILED AS REGISTERED, AND THE CHECK WAS DEFECTIVE. I registered that the warm-epoch
    core clock must exceed 4.0 GHz in every arm, and wrote that if it failed, nothing else in
    the registration could be read. The lowest arm is 3.537 GHz. By the letter of the
    registration this run is unreadable.

    The letter is wrong, and the reason does not depend on the outcome. The clock is a steady
    state and not a ramp: across the three epochs of every arm its spread is between 1.000x
    and 1.135x, and eleven of the thirteen arms are inside 1.02x. What it tracks is the
    DURATION of an epoch, not the message count:

        arm                                  ms per epoch    GHz
        acqrel/pad128/uncached/isb                  507.0  4.332
        seqcst/pad128/uncached/isb                  509.6  4.287
        acqrel/pad64/uncached/isb                   421.4  4.390
        fenced/pad128/uncached/isb                  226.3  4.374
        fenced/pad128/cached/isb                     51.8  3.979
        acqrel/pad128/cached/isb                     23.3  3.561
        seqcst/pad128/cached/isb                     22.1  3.785

    Every arm processes exactly ten million messages. The cached arms finish in twenty-three
    milliseconds because they are twenty-two times faster per message, and twenty-three
    milliseconds is not long enough for the frequency governor to reach peak. So a threshold
    stated per message cannot control a phenomenon that depends on duration, and demanding
    that every arm reach peak clock at a fixed message count is demanding something impossible
    of arms that differ by twenty-two times in speed.

    What the check was for was to establish that the run is long enough to have ramped, after
    H67 found a 200,000-message run measured at 1.085 GHz. On that question the answer is yes
    and it is not close: the lowest arm here is 3.537 GHz, 3.26 times the idle clock, and every
    uncached arm, which is where the disputed figure lives, is between 4.271 and 4.396 GHz.

    The honest handling: part (d) is recorded as failed, the check is recorded as defective
    with the reason above, and parts (a), (b) and (c) are read with their clocks stated beside
    them. Reinterpreting a failed pre-registered check after seeing the data is the move
    pre-registration exists to prevent, so the defect is named in terms of the harness rather
    than in terms of what the other parts showed, and the corrected check for any future run
    is stated here: every arm's epoch duration must exceed 200 ms, which is a duration
    threshold for a duration phenomenon.

(a) SUPPORTED. The registered claim was that SeqCst and AcqRel cost within 10 percent of each
    other per message when the index is not cached, in cycles and in nanoseconds. At 128-byte
    padding, performance cores, 30 launches:

        cycles       AcqRel 221.070 [219.890, 221.880]   SeqCst 220.445 [217.030, 221.760]
                     ratio 0.997
        nanoseconds  AcqRel  50.701 [ 50.542,  51.467]   SeqCst  51.032 [ 50.698,  51.529]
                     ratio 1.007

    So the RCsc acquire load costs the same as the RCpc one in an uncached SPSC ring at a
    realistic run length, to within three parts in a thousand in cycles and seven in
    nanoseconds. The 6.79x this work published, measured at 200,000 messages, is confined to
    run lengths where the core has not ramped. RQ1's uncached answer is that the ordering
    costs nothing.

(b) SUPPORTED, and it is the result a reviewer will disbelieve. The registered claim was that
    Fenced costs below 0.60 of AcqRel uncached, inverting the published ranking. It costs
    0.4475 in cycles (98.935 against 221.070) and 0.4464 in nanoseconds (22.632 against
    50.701). H67's two-epoch sweep put this at 0.447 at the same message count, so two runs
    with different epoch counts agree to one part in a thousand.

    Stated plainly: adding two `dmb ish` instructions per ring operation makes the uncached
    loop 2.23 times faster. The explicit barrier is not a cost here, it is the fastest of the
    three orderings by a wide margin, and it is the only one whose cost does not grow with run
    length (98.9 cycles here against 86.3 at 200,000 messages, while AcqRel goes 21.9 to
    221.1).

THE CORRECTED ANSWER TO RQ1, which is what this run exists to produce.

    Between the two acquire-load forms there is no difference worth measuring, at either
    caching setting, at a realistic run length: 0.979 cached and 0.997 uncached. The
    distinction between `ldapr` and `ldar` that the instruction census confirmed is in the
    binary does not show up in the cost.

    The explicit double barrier is a different matter and it flips sign. `dmb ish` either side
    of a plain load costs 2.45 times AcqRel when the consumer caches the producer's index, and
    0.45 times AcqRel when it does not. The same instruction sequence, the same ring, the same
    machine: 2.45x more expensive in one configuration and 2.23x cheaper in the other.

    That is the finding, and the mechanism is not established. What is ruled out is the
    obvious explanation: Fenced carries MORE L1D misses than AcqRel in every configuration
    (3.785 against 3.031 per message uncached at pad=128, 1.418 against 0.913 cached) and is
    nonetheless faster uncached, so cache behaviour does not account for it. A plausible story
    is that the barrier prevents a speculative behaviour the acquire path permits, and that the
    behaviour only becomes pathological once the stream is long enough for the coherence
    traffic to accumulate, but nothing measured here tests that.

PADDING ALSO DEPENDS ON RUN LENGTH, which H61 could not have seen. Paired inside each launch,
cycles per message, positive meaning 64-byte padding is cheaper:

    ordering   configuration   H61 at 200,000 messages      H68 at 10,000,000 messages
    AcqRel     uncached        16 of 30, p = 0.8555, none   26 of 30, p = 0.0001, +36.865
    SeqCst     uncached        29 of 30, strong             27 of 30, p < 0.0001, +35.820
    Fenced     uncached        23 of 30, strong             14 of 30, p = 0.8555, none
    AcqRel     cached          not reported here            27 of 30 favour pad=128, -0.375
    SeqCst     cached          not reported here            24 of 30 favour pad=128, -0.685
    Fenced     cached          not reported here            18 of 30, p = 0.3616, none

H61 concluded that pad=64 being faster in uncached streaming reproduces for SeqCst and Fenced
and not at all for AcqRel. At ten million messages it reproduces for AcqRel and SeqCst and not
at all for Fenced. Two of the three per-ordering answers reverse. Sharing one coherence granule
is worth about 17 percent of the uncached cost for both acquire forms and nothing for the
barrier; with the index cached the separate granule wins instead, by 4 to 8 percent.

And a counterintuitive detail that rules out the easy explanation here too: pad=64 carries MORE
L1D misses than pad=128 in every single case, by 0.009 to 0.512 per message, and is
nonetheless the faster arrangement uncached. Whatever sharing the granule buys, it is not
fewer misses.

WHAT THIS RUN DOES NOT SETTLE. Why AcqRel's uncached cost grows tenfold with run length while
Fenced's grows by 15 percent. L1D misses are ruled out as the explanation in both directions.
The measurement that would settle it is a counter sweep on the uncached AcqRel arm across run
lengths reading store-buffer and coherence events rather than cache misses, and whether the M4
PMU exposes the needed events is not known here. Until that exists this work states the
effect, states its size in both units, states that two independent runs agree on it, and
states that the cause is open.
