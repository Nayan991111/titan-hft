STEP 1, the check RQ1 depended on. The instruction census on the measurement machine shows
what the compiler actually emitted for the ring's index operations:

    titan_census_pop_acqrel    ldapr=1   stlur=1
    titan_census_pop_seqcst    ldar=1    stlur=1
    titan_census_pop_fenced    dmb=2
    titan_census_push_acqrel   ldapur=1  stlr=1
    titan_census_push_seqcst   ldar=1    stlr=1
    titan_census_push_fenced   dmb=2
    titan_census_wait_wfe      ldxr=1  sevl=1  wfe=2

So the three policies really are the three forms RQ1 asks about: the RCpc acquire load
(`ldapr`, and its unscaled-offset form `ldapur`), the RCsc acquire load (`ldar`), and a plain
load with an explicit `dmb ish` either side. Releases are `stlr` and its unscaled form
`stlur`. The wait path uses the armed exclusive monitor, `ldxr` then `sevl` then `wfe`, which
is the correct idiom and not a bare `wfe` that could sleep forever. Without this census the
paper's RQ1 would have rested on a documentation comment; with it, the claim is about
instructions that are provably in the binary.

A DEFECT IN THIS HYPOTHESIS'S OWN REGISTRATION, stated before the verdicts because it changes
how three of them must be read. Parts (b), (c) and (e) each name a count or a bound without
naming the configuration or the metric it applies to. The harness measures 16 cases across
two QoS classes and reports both cycles and nanoseconds, and for all three parts the answer
differs by configuration or by metric. That is my error in writing the registration, not a
property of the data. Each part below is therefore reported under every reading the wording
admits, with the full per-configuration counts, and nothing is selected after the fact.

H64 (a) REFUTED. The registered claim had two halves: the SeqCst to AcqRel ratio of
    round-trip cycles inside 0.90 to 1.10 with the consumer caching the producer's index, and
    above 1.10 without it. The first half holds, the second fails.
        median of per-launch ratios, caching ON : 1.000, 0.998, 1.041, 1.001
        median of per-launch ratios, caching OFF: 0.999, 0.997, 1.014, 0.955
    Every one of the eight is inside 0.90 to 1.10. The ordering makes no difference to the
    cross-core round trip whether the index is cached or not.

    This is the result worth the paper, and it is a contrast rather than a null. In the
    streaming harness, measured under the same protocol on the same machine, SeqCst costs
    6.79 and 3.79 times AcqRel when the index is not cached. Here it costs between 0.955 and
    1.041 times. The same three orderings, the same ring, the same silicon, and the ordering
    is a 6.8x effect in one harness and nothing in the other.

    The explanation the numbers support: the cross-core round trip costs 452 to 510 cycles,
    and that cost is the coherence transfer between two cores, which no choice of acquire
    form changes. The ordering instruction is a few cycles inside a several-hundred-cycle
    round trip. Streaming has no such floor, so throughput there is limited by the ordering
    instructions themselves and they dominate. Ordering cost is therefore visible in a
    throughput-bound pipeline and invisible in a latency-bound handoff, and reporting either
    one alone would mislead.

(b) SUPPORTED on performance cores, reversed on efficiency cores. On USER_INTERACTIVE, isb
    spinning costs more than wfe in 30 of 30 launches, sign p < 0.0001: 351.55 against 271.15
    cycles, so wfe is 1.30x cheaper. On BACKGROUND the direction flips completely, isb costing
    more in 0 of 30 launches: 333.50 against 353.55 cycles, so wfe is 1.06x more expensive.
    The registration named no QoS class; the default mode of the harness is USER_INTERACTIVE
    and that is the reading under which the part is marked supported, with the reversal on the
    efficiency cores recorded as part of the result and not as a footnote.

(c) REGISTRATION AMBIGUOUS, and the two readings disagree. The part asks whether the
    BACKGROUND to USER_INTERACTIVE round-trip ratio is below 3, without saying in which unit.
        in cycles:    median 0.932 across configurations, range 0.808 to 1.298  -> below 3
        in wall time: median 3.057 across configurations, range 2.664 to 3.939  -> above 3
    Both are computed from the same 60 launches. The part is therefore not verdicted as
    written. What it uncovered is more useful than the verdict would have been.

    The efficiency core runs at 1.080 GHz in every single BACKGROUND case, with a bootstrap
    interval of [1.080, 1.080] over 30 launches, while the performance core runs at 3.506 to
    3.628 GHz. That is a clock ratio of about 3.28. Against it, the efficiency core completes
    the round trip in FEWER cycles than the performance core, 0.808 to 0.984 of them, and in
    about three times the wall time. Fewer cycles, far more nanoseconds.

    This is the clearest case in the whole project for reporting both units, which the
    protocol has done throughout. A cycles-only paper would conclude the efficiency cores are
    slightly better at cross-core handoff. A nanoseconds-only paper would conclude they are
    three times worse and would not know why. Both are true statements about the same runs.

    It also corrects H8, which claimed the efficiency core round trip is at least 3x the
    performance core and was refuted by a preliminary run with no results file. Under this
    protocol the wall-time ratio is 3.057, so H8's claim holds in time, marginally, and fails
    in cycles. H8 is not re-verdicted here because its own run is unquotable, but the question
    it asked now has an answer from 60 launches on wall power.

(d) SUPPORTED. Under SeqCst without index caching, pad=64 is cheaper than pad=128 in 28 of 30
    launches on efficiency cores and 29 of 30 on performance cores, both sign p < 0.0001. The
    streaming finding reproduces across cores, and more broadly than registered: pad=64 is
    also cheaper at 24 to 30 of 30 in six further configurations. Two configurations reverse,
    fenced uncached on performance cores (5 of 30, p = 0.0003) and SeqCst cached on efficiency
    cores (6 of 30, p = 0.0014), so sharing one coherence granule is usually but not always
    the cheaper arrangement.

(e) SUPPORTED in seven of eight configurations, with the exception named. Fenced costs more
    than AcqRel at 24 to 30 of 30 launches in seven configurations, sign p from 0.0014 down to
    below 0.0001. The eighth, pad=128 uncached on performance cores, shows no effect at 12 of
    30 and p = 0.3616. The registration again named no configuration, so this is reported as
    seven of eight rather than as a single verdict. The largest penalty is pad=128 cached on
    performance cores, where Fenced costs 1.250 times AcqRel.

RQ3, and the harness answered it more directly than the registered parts did. Involuntary
context switches over 200,000 round trips, median across launches:

    lock-free ring, performance cores          0        maximum observed  89
    lock-free ring, efficiency cores           1 to 3   maximum observed 516
    mutex baseline, performance cores         36        maximum observed 603,073
    mutex baseline, efficiency cores      53,658        maximum observed 1,229,115

XNU does not preempt the two spinning lock-free threads on the performance cores at all in
the median launch, across every ordering and every wait strategy. The mutex baseline on the
same machine in the same launches is descheduled tens of thousands of times. The lock-free
ring's advantage over a mutex on this scheduler is therefore not only its 2 to 3 times lower
round trip, 271 to 510 cycles against 1,075, but that it is not handed back to the scheduler
at all.

A number the paper must not get wrong. The performance core does not run at 4.4 GHz during
cross-core ping-pong. Measured across 30 launches it runs at 3.506 to 3.628 GHz, about 80% of
the figure a datasheet-derived calculation would use. Any nanosecond claim converted from
cycles must use the measured clock of the arm it came from, and every table here reports
core_ghz per case so that conversion is always checkable.
