E2: BARRIER-PLACEMENT CROSS ON VIRTUALIZED x86-64, AS A CONTROL FOR H69
EXPLORATORY, NOT PRE-REGISTERED. Run 2026-11-17.
POWER   : not applicable, virtualized x86-64 host with no battery and no accessible power state

PURPOSE. H69 asks which half of an acquire-release pair carries the Fenced
inversion measured on the M4 (0.4475 uncached, 2.449 cached, both reproduced).
Before spending a 40-minute measurement on the measuring machine, the same
harness was run on a machine where the answer is predictable from the memory
model, to check that the harness and the statistics behave as expected. They
did not behave as expected, and this file is why the H69 registration was
revised before the M4 run rather than after it.

HOST. Linux x86_64 in a KVM-virtualized Docker container. Intel Xeon, family 6
model 207 stepping 2, 2 vCPUs, 96 KiB L1d and 4 MiB L2 across the two, 260 MiB
L3 reported (a slice of a far larger socket, shared with tenants this run
cannot see). rustc 1.97.0. NO PMU: no cycles, no instructions, no cache
misses, no core clock. Every figure below is wall time. No cpufreq interface
exists in the container, so frequency could not be observed even in principle.

BINARY. target/release/titan-barrier at this commit. Five orderings at
pad=128, each cached and uncached, ten cases:

    acqrel       ldapr-equivalent   stlr-equivalent   (plain movs on x86)
    fencedload   relaxed + fence    release store
    fencedstore  acquire load       fence + relaxed store
    fenced       relaxed + fence    fence + relaxed store
    unordered    relaxed            relaxed           (NOT a correct ring)

PROTOCOL. 30 independent launches of titan-barrier 10000000 2, launch label
injected per line, each launch reduced to its per-case median across epochs,
comparisons paired inside each launch, exact two-sided sign test. 600 data
lines. Every ordered arm reported sum_ok=1 in all 30 launches, so no ordered
arm lost or duplicated a message. The unordered arm also reported sum_ok=1
throughout, which says nothing about its correctness: it is permitted to
reorder and simply did not do so observably on this host.

RESULT 1: UNCACHED, THE TWO BARRIER SIDES COST THE SAME, AND THEY SATURATE.
Nanoseconds per message, paired, positive favouring the barrier arm:

    acqrel against fencedload    median diff -64.637 [-68.017, -62.190]
                                 ratio 0.212, acqrel cheaper 30/30
    acqrel against fencedstore   median diff -65.053 [-68.975, -60.820]
                                 ratio 0.207, acqrel cheaper 30/30
    acqrel against fenced        median diff -92.183 [-93.297, -87.772]
                                 ratio 0.150, acqrel cheaper 30/30

The two sides cost within 0.7 percent of each other (64.637 against 65.053),
and both together cost 92.183 rather than the 129.690 they would cost if they
added. Store-side share 0.7098, load-side share 0.7102, sum 1.4145, within
0.15 of 1 in only 3 of 30 launches. So on this host the barrier cost SATURATES:
a second fence per operation adds about 42 percent of what the first one cost.
This is the expected behaviour on a machine where acquire-release is a plain
move and a fence is a real instruction, and it is the reference the M4 numbers
are read against.

RESULT 2, AND IT WAS NOT EXPECTED: A WEAK INVERSION APPEARS HERE TOO, CACHED.
Cached, nanoseconds per message, paired:

    acqrel against fencedload    median diff +2.460 [+0.391, +4.682]
                                 ratio 1.142, acqrel LARGER in 23/30, p=0.0052
    acqrel against fencedstore   ratio 0.398, acqrel cheaper 30/30
    acqrel against fenced        ratio 0.418, acqrel cheaper 30/30

Adding a fence after a relaxed load, with the release store left alone, makes
the cached loop 14 percent CHEAPER than the acquire load on this x86 host,
significant at 23 of 30. On x86 an acquire load is a plain move, so FencedLoad
strictly adds an instruction and should strictly cost more. It does not.

THIS MATTERS FOR THE PAPER'S FRAMING AND IT CONSTRAINS A CLAIM THE PAPER WAS
ABOUT TO MAKE. The M4 inversion was being written up as a property of Apple
silicon. A weak inversion in the same direction appears on x86-64, in the other
caching configuration, at 1.14x against the M4's 2.23x. The honest statement is
therefore NOT that the inversion is Apple-specific. It is that a barrier can be
cheaper than the ordered instruction it replaces on both architectures
measured, that the effect is an order of magnitude larger on the M4 and appears
in the opposite caching configuration, and that this is consistent with the
cause being an interaction between barrier placement and producer-consumer
interleaving rather than the cost of an instruction.

WHY THAT INTERPRETATION, AND WHY IT IS NOT A CONCLUSION. The cached-side
numbers on this host are the fastest arms and therefore the most sensitive to
preemption, and preemption here is severe: involuntary context switch counts
up to 1133 per epoch on a 2-vCPU container running a producer, a consumer and
the operating system with no spare core. The 23 of 30 result may be real or may
be an artifact of that. It cannot be settled here. It is recorded because it
changes what the paper may claim, not because it settles anything.

RESULT 3: THE UNORDERED ARM IS NOT A COST FLOOR, AND H69 (f) WAS WRONG.
Uncached, (acqrel - unordered) / acqrel has median -0.3596 [-0.7430, -0.2321]
and is at or above the registered 0.30 threshold in 1 of 30 launches. The sign
is NEGATIVE: removing all ordering makes the uncached loop about 36 percent
SLOWER, not faster, and acqrel is cheaper than unordered in 24 of 30 launches
(p = 0.0014). Cached the difference is 0.0324 and not significant (17/30,
p = 0.5847).

An arm that is slower than the arms it is supposed to bound is not a floor.
Removing the ordering does not measure "the same loop without ordering"; it
measures a different loop, in which the producer is no longer held in step and
the two sides interleave differently. H69 (f) was registered as "ordering is at
or above 0.30 of the loop, measured against the Unordered floor", and that
hypothesis is not merely refuted, it is MALFORMED: there is no floor to measure
against. It is rewritten before the M4 run as a validity check on the arm
itself rather than as a claim about the loop.

RESULT 4: H69 (g) WOULD HAVE FAILED ON THE M4 FOR THE FOURTH TIME.
Shortest epoch here is 86.559 ms, in the unordered cached arm, and 222 of 300
arm-launches exceed 200 ms. The cached arms are roughly 20 times faster per
message than the uncached arms by construction, so at a fixed message count
they always run short. On the M4 the cached arms finish 10M messages in about
23 ms. A single global 200 ms threshold across all arms therefore fails on a
correct run, which is exactly how H68 (d) failed. The check is rewritten to
apply to the uncached arms, where the result lives, and to record the cached
arms' durations without a pass or fail. Noted plainly: this is the fourth
version of this check, and the recurring error is writing one global threshold
for a quantity that differs per arm by design.

WHAT THIS FILE IS FOR, AND WHAT IT IS NOT FOR. It is the control that shows the
harness, the pairing and the sign test behave on a machine whose memory model
predicts the answer, and it is the reason three parts of the H69 registration
were corrected before any M4 time was spent. It is NOT evidence about Apple
silicon, it is NOT a cross-architecture claim, and its cached-side numbers are
not trustworthy enough to quote without the preemption caveat attached. Limits
1 through 7 recorded in results/2026-11-16_x86_crossarch.txt apply here in
full, the first of them being that frequency is unobservable from inside this
container.
